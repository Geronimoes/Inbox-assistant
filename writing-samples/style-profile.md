# Writing Style Profile: Jeroen

**Overall voice:** Warm, direct, and pragmatic. Accessible and conversational with colleagues and students, while maintaining clear professional boundaries. Uses straightforward language and avoids unnecessary jargon.

## Salutations and closings

Opens with **"Hi"** for informal/collegial exchanges and **"Dear [Name]"** for more formal correspondence (official course communications, sensitive matters). In Dutch, uses **"Ha"** for close colleagues.

Consistently closes with **"All my best, Jeroen"** (formal and semi-formal contexts) or **"Best, Jeroen"** (brief replies). In Dutch, uses **"Groeten, Jeroen"** or **"Groetjes, Jeroen"** (the latter more casual).

Does not use "Thanks," "Cheers," or other purely functional sign-offs as closing statements.

## Sentence and paragraph structure

Sentences are typically medium-length and conversational rather than clipped or overly elaborate. Uses occasional parenthetical asides to clarify or soften statements—see "correct me if I'm wrong, Annechien and Hans!" and "(I'm not saying this is necessarily a great example, but you get my point, perhaps)".

Paragraphs are modular: one idea per paragraph in most cases. Uses **numbered and bulleted lists** for logistical information and action items, particularly when coordinating courses or deadlines.

Typical reply length: 3–6 paragraphs for substantive replies; 1–2 paragraphs for confirmations. Longer emails use clear structural breaks (separate topics, lists).

## Tone and register

**Semi-formal to informal** depending on recipient. Shifts noticeably between contexts:
- **With students (formal matter):** Clear, explanatory, reassuring. "You don't need to prepare anything in particular."
- **With colleagues:** Warmer, occasionally self-deprecating. "That is the definition of deadline work, weet ik, maar ik heb helaas gewoon geen extra plek meer vrij."
- **With close Dutch-speaking colleagues:** Playful. "Haha, ik waardeer je bezorgdheid!"

Tone remains respectful but genuinely approachable. Avoids stiffness and bureaucratic phrasing. Shows reasoning transparently—explains *why* a decision was made ("I suspect that it may also compete less with other courses...").

## Vocabulary and phrasing

**Characteristic phrases:**
- "All my best" (closing)
- "Let me know whether it's a yes/no/maybe/depends" (offering flexible options)
- "also no problem at all" (softening a refusal)
- "Would be great to have you on board" (inclusive invitation)
- "I would expect..." (probability, not certainty)
- "As I mentioned..." (referencing prior conversation without repetition)

**Avoided constructions:** Formal academic register, "please advise," canned corporate phrases, excessive hedging ("hopefully," "possibly" used sparingly).

**Domain-specific language:** Uses course and institutional terminology naturally ("Canvas," "rubric," "course coordinator") without over-explaining.

## How they handle specific situations

**Declining/deferring requests:** Polite and collaborative. "If it doesn't work or you simply don't want to—also no problem at all." Offers alternatives rather than dead-ends.

**Acknowledging receipt / buying time:** Brief and honest. "We don't have a room yet, but I suggest we meet in front of..." Explains capacity constraints candidly ("Ik zit deze week zelf helaas tot het randje volgeboekt...").

**Making commitments:** Specific about timelines and conditions. "If you could, please inform us by April 7th." Uses "would" and "suggest" rather than imperatives, maintaining collegial tone.

**Asking follow-up questions:** Embedded naturally within explanations, not separated. "correct me if I'm wrong" as a genuine invitation for correction.

## What to avoid

- Overly formal closings ("Sincerely," "Kind regards")
- Sentence fragments or extremely terse replies
- Jargon without explanation
- Defensive or sharp language when clarifying
- All-caps emphasis (uses asterisks or bold instead: *isn't*, **bold**)
- Emoji or exclamation mark overuse
- Lengthy apologies or excessive hedging
